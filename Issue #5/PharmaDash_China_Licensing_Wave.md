# Why Big Pharma Is Quietly Sourcing Its Next Blockbusters From China

*Western drugmakers spent a record $137 billion licensing drugs from Chinese biotechs in 2025 — and 2026 is already bigger. It's the most important commercial shift in pharma that most people aren't talking about. Here's what's happening, and why Washington is nervous.*

---

Here's a number that should stop you: in 2025, Chinese biotech companies signed a record **$137.7 billion** in out-licensing deals with global drugmakers — nearly ten times the $13.9 billion they did in 2021.

For years, "made in China" pharma meant cheap manufacturing and generic copies. That era is over. Today, Western pharma is increasingly *buying its next generation of drugs* from Chinese labs — paying billions for the global rights to molecules discovered in Shanghai, Suzhou, and Shenyang.

This is arguably the most consequential commercial dynamic in the industry right now, and it connects directly to the two forces this newsletter has already covered: the patent cliff forcing pharma to refill pipelines, and the hunt for cheaper, faster innovation. China has quietly become the answer to both. Let's break it down.

---

## The scale: from a trickle to a torrent

The trajectory is almost vertical. Aggregate deal value with Chinese biotechs went from roughly $32 billion in 2022 to $35 billion in 2023, jumped to $52 billion in 2024, and then exploded to a record in 2025. Deal *count* tells the same story: Greater China firms completed 186 cross-border out-licensing deals in 2025, up from 65 the year before.

And 2026 is on pace to shatter that record. By one measure, Chinese biotechs struck **$60 billion in cross-border deals in the first quarter of 2026 alone** — accounting for roughly two-thirds of the total value of global biotech dealmaking in that period.

China's share of global out-licensing value has climbed from 21% across 2023–24 to roughly a third of all industry licensing spending. Put simply: **about one in four newly licensed drug assets now originates in China.**

## The marquee deals

The abstract numbers become real in the headline transactions. A few that define the wave:

**Pfizer and 3SBio** — the deal that broke the mold. In mid-2025 Pfizer paid a record **$1.25 billion upfront** (plus a $100 million equity stake and up to $4.8 billion in milestones) for the ex-China rights to SSGJ-707, a PD-1/VEGF bispecific antibody for cancer. That upfront was the largest ever for a Chinese-origin therapy.

**AstraZeneca and CSPC** — the obesity bet. After an initial AI-driven research pact, AstraZeneca went back to Hebei-based CSPC in 2026 for weight-loss and metabolic drug candidates in a deal worth **up to $18.5 billion**, including $1.2 billion upfront. That ties directly to the obesity war covered in an earlier issue — even the GLP-1 gold rush is now sourcing from China.

**GSK and Hengrui** — the portfolio play. GSK put down $500 million upfront for a COPD candidate plus options on eleven more programs, a package worth up to **$12 billion** if it delivers.

Others fill out the picture: AbbVie licensed RemeGen's cancer drug for up to $5.6 billion, Sanofi took a Sino Biopharm asset, Takeda fronted Innovent $1.2 billion, and Lilly expanded its work with Insilico Medicine. This is no longer opportunistic. As one UBS analyst put it, China-originated assets are now treated as core strategic pipelines rather than bolt-ons.

## Why China, and why now

The logic is brutally simple, and it's about cost, speed, and quality converging at once.

Chinese assets have historically carried upfronts **60–70% lower** than Western equivalents, with total deal sizes 40–50% smaller. For a big pharma company staring down a patent cliff, that's a clinical-stage drug at a discount it simply couldn't match at home.

But the real shift is that the discount now comes *without* a quality penalty. China's regulator has overhauled its approval pathways, and the country's R&D machine runs faster and cheaper than Western norms. The proof is in the pipeline: China now accounts for around **30% of the world's novel drug candidates**, nearly level with the US, and in 2025 more drugs made their global debut in China than anywhere else — the first time any country has overtaken the US on that metric.

Even Pfizer's CEO said the quiet part out loud in 2026, warning that for the first time in decades, US biotech dominance is being genuinely challenged — by China.

The deal structure also reveals the caution baked in. Most buyers take **ex-China rights with an option back into the mainland** — a design that hedges the political risk of owning Chinese clinical operations outright. That structure is a tell, and it points to the elephant in the room.

---

## The catch: Washington is watching

This is where the story gets complicated, and where a serious analysis has to sit with the tension rather than resolve it.

In December 2025, the US enacted the **BIOSECURE Act** (folded into the annual defense bill), restricting federal dealings with certain Chinese biotech firms on national-security grounds — data security, supply-chain dependence, and the fear that, as one former FDA chief warned, "every drug could be made in China." On top of that, the US announced steep **Section 232 tariffs on patented pharmaceuticals**, phasing in through 2026.

The dilemma is genuine, and the industry is openly split from its own government. Pharma sees China as an indispensable pool of innovation to refill pipelines facing an estimated **$230 billion in patent-cliff losses** by 2030. Washington sees a strategic dependence to be reduced. Critics of the crackdown — including major biotech investors — warn that if US firms alone are barred from tapping Chinese efficiency, they'll simply become less competitive than European and Japanese rivals who face no such limits.

There's a structural irony worth noting: today's deals actually favor the West commercially. Chinese partners typically keep only 10–15% royalties on ex-China sales — less than a third of the profit. So restricting these deals may hurt American drugmakers' pipelines more than it hurts China's balance sheet.

---

## What to watch

The momentum looks durable. A BofA analyst predicts the total value of China licensing-out deals will double again over the next 18–24 months, and Western venture firms are now building entire companies around China-sourced molecules — one such obesity startup, built on Hengrui assets, recently landed one of the biggest biotech IPOs ever.

The open questions are political, not commercial. Whether the BIOSECURE Act's designations expand, whether tariffs make ex-China sourcing uneconomical, and whether China retaliates with export controls will shape the next phase. Some analysts think biotech is one breakthrough — a "DeepSeek moment" — away from becoming the next front in US-China rivalry.

For now, the commercial verdict is already in. The world's most valuable drug pipelines are increasingly being sourced from China, and the companies that master this cross-border corridor — bridging Asian innovation with Western capital and reach — will have a structural edge for the rest of the decade. The only real uncertainty is how much their own governments will let them.
