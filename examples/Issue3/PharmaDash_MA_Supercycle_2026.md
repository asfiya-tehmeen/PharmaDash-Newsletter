# Why Big Pharma Is Spending Billions to Buy Its Way Out of the Patent Cliff 

*$106 billion spent in five months, a $400 billion patent cliff looming, and $1.3 trillion still in the war chest. Here's what's driving the most aggressive dealmaking pharma has seen in seven years, and what it means commercially.*

---

By early June, pharma companies had already announced roughly **$106 billion in M&A across 201 deals** in 2026, according to PitchBook. The average deal has ballooned to **$527 million**, up from $365 million last year. One HSBC analyst summed up the mood bluntly: companies are "really buying stuff like it's going out of fashion."

If the pace holds, 2026 is on track to clear **$250 billion** in deal value, the strongest year for biotech and pharma since the 2019 pre-Covid peak. After 2024 (just $114.8 billion) and 2025 (around $209 billion), the industry has shifted from cautious to full-throttle acquisition.

For anyone tracking the commercial side of pharma, this is what's happening. The obesity war grabs the headlines, but M&A is where the next decade of revenue is quietly being bought today. Here's why it's happening, who's spending, and what it signals.

---

## By the numbers: the scale of the surge

The momentum is visible at every level of the funnel:

- **$106B** in announced M&A across **201 deals** so far in 2026 (PitchBook, early June)
- **$84B** of that came in just the first four months, versus $24.5 billion in upfront value over the same stretch of 2025
- **7 deals over $1 billion** were announced in the final 12 days of March alone
- **$77.3B** in biopharma licensing value was announced in Q1, though only about 6% of that was upfront cash, the rest tied to milestones
- **~$1.3 trillion** in collective deal capacity sits on Big Pharma balance sheets, most of it still undeployed

> **Dashboard idea:** An annual deal-value bar chart from 2022 to 2026 (with 2026 shown as a projected $250B) tells the "supercycle" story in one frame — the trough, the rebound, and the breakout. Add a line for average deal size to show deals aren't just more frequent, they're bigger.

A note of caution on the headline number: trackers disagree. IQVIA's full-year forecast is more conservative at **$140 - 160 billion**, with upside to $190 billion. The gap reflects different methodologies, but it's a reminder to treat any single "total" as a directional signal.

---

## The engine: a $400 billion patent cliff

Strip away the noise and almost every deal traces back to one number: the wall of patent expirations coming between now and 2030.

Industry estimates put **$200 - 400 billion in branded drug revenue at direct risk** of loss of exclusivity (LOE) this decade. And unlike the early-2010s cliff, which mostly hit primary-care pills like Lipitor, this one targets the industry's most profitable biologics and specialty franchises:

- **Keytruda** (Merck), the world's former best-selling drug at roughly $29.5 billion a year, begins its US decline in January 2028 and loses core exclusivity around December 2028.
- **Opdivo** (Bristol Myers Squibb) hits its US exclusivity wall in 2028.
- **Eliquis** (BMS/Pfizer), a $10 billion-plus anticoagulant, faces patent expiry between 2027 and 2029.
- **Ibrance** (Pfizer) and **Trulicity** (Lilly) reach exclusivity thresholds in 2027.

When a small-molecule bestseller meets generic competition, sales can fall 80 - 90% within a year. That's the hole executives are racing to fill. As the same HSBC analyst put it, you still have to fill the gap in the P&L when the cliffs hit, and you can't grow a new $10 billion franchise organically in 18 months. So you buy one.

## Who's buying: the biggest deals of 2026

The year's transactions span oncology, metabolic disease, radiopharmaceuticals, cell therapy, and CNS, a notable breadth that signals this isn't a one-therapy gold rush.

- **Sun Pharma → Organon: ~$11.75 billion.** The largest deal of the year so far, and a statement: India's Sun Pharma acquiring a New Jersey-based women's health and biosimilars company in an all-cash deal. The biggest check of 2026 came from outside the traditional Western establishment.
- **Lantheus → Curium: ~$7 billion.** A bet on radiopharmaceuticals, one of oncology's fastest-growing frontiers.
- **Merck → Terns: $6.7 billion.** Merck continues to spend ahead of the Keytruda cliff.
- **Gilead → Arcellx: ~$7 billion.** Doubling down on cell therapy.
- **Eli Lilly → Orna Therapeutics: $2.4 billion.** One of *four* acquisitions Lilly disclosed in Q1 alone, bringing in an in-vivo CAR-T platform built on circular RNA. Even the industry's fastest-growing company is buying aggressively.

The common thread, per dealmakers, is a focus on assets that are either near-commercial, plugging revenue gaps fast, or early-stage platforms that buy access to new technology. Oncology remains the single biggest target, accounting for roughly **39%** of deal activity.

## Why the floodgates opened now

The patent cliff explains the *motive*, but several forces unlocked the *means*:

The biotech equity market has roared back. The XBI biotech index is up about **64%** over the past year, giving targets healthier valuations and sellers more confidence. A friendlier FTC under the Trump administration, combined with Federal Reserve rate cuts, removed two of the biggest brakes on large transactions that had chilled dealmaking through early 2025.

There's one competitive wildcard worth flagging: **licensing deals with Chinese biotechs**. Western pharma is increasingly accessing innovation from China through partnerships rather than acquisitions - a faster, cheaper route that poses a genuine alternative to traditional M&A. (That's a story big enough for its own issue, and it's coming!)

---

## What to watch

The firepower is far from spent. With roughly $1.3 trillion in deal capacity still largely undeployed and the steepest part of the patent cliff arriving in 2028, the pressure to keep buying will only intensify over the next two years.

Three things worth tracking from here: whether a true megadeal (north of $25 billion) finally closes, whether oncology's dominance gives way to more obesity and CNS dealmaking, and whether China licensing starts to siphon capital that would otherwise fund Western acquisitions.

One thing is already clear. The patent cliff isn't a future risk anymore, it's a present problem.
